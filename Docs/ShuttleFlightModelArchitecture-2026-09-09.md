# Shuttle-derived flight-model architecture

Date: 2026-09-09

This note records which parts of the current KSP Shuttle Lander vessel-model,
calibration and energy-management architecture were changed after reviewing the
Space Shuttle program's operational aerodynamic, entry-guidance, flight-control
and post-flight identification methods.

## What the Orbiter actually did

The Shuttle's operational aerodynamic model was a preflight database, not an
onboard self-training model. Rockwell's Operational Aerodynamic Data Book was
the operational reference for full-scale aerodynamic performance, stability
and control. The database itself came from extensive wind-tunnel development
and was revised using reviewed Shuttle flight-test results.

During entry the onboard guidance did not estimate a new aerodynamic model from
each telemetry frame. Entry guidance followed a precomputed/reference drag
acceleration profile versus velocity and generated bank and angle-of-attack
commands to bring the actual trajectory back to that reference while respecting
heating, load and terminal constraints.

The flight-control system likewise used scheduled control allocation rather
than one fixed control law. Early entry was RCS dominated. Aerodynamic surfaces
were blended in as dynamic pressure increased and the RCS axes were progressively
retired; historical Shuttle documentation identifies roll RCS retirement before
pitch, with yaw RCS retained to roughly Mach 1. TAEM and approach/landing were
all-aerodynamic flight-control regimes.

TAEM was explicitly an energy-management system. The Shuttle controlled altitude
and dynamic-pressure/energy profiles against range-to-go, used S-turns and the
HAC to dispose of excess energy, and used angle of attack and speedbrake as
separate energy/path effectors. NASA TM-104744 documents the TAEM speedbrake
controller as primarily proportional-plus-integral feedback on q-bar error with
an additional energy term for cases in which q-bar and total energy disagree.

Aerodynamic refinement happened through flight test and post-flight analysis.
Dedicated maneuvers were designed to excite useful dynamics, and modified/
maximum-likelihood parameter estimation was used to extract stability, control,
lift and drag information from recorded flight data. Those results were compared
with preflight predictions to reduce uncertainty and improve later operational
certification; they were not blindly promoted during the same entry that
generated them.

Operational planning also treated aerodynamic uncertainty explicitly. The
practical lesson is to keep a nominal reviewed model stable and evaluate
off-nominal aerodynamic/atmospheric cases around it rather than letting noisy
online identification continuously move the nominal trajectory underneath the
guidance system.

## KSP implementation adopted here

### 1. Prior-flight aerodynamic data book

`Runtime/Physics/observations.sqlite3` remains the raw flight-test archive. A
new bridge session may seed the native model only from matching structure and
environment observations generated by previous sessions/timelines. The current
session is excluded from its own data-book payload.

Historical observations are reduced into local cells over q, Mach, AoA,
sideslip and discrete configuration state. Each cell first selects one clean
observation per independent session/timeline. The operational representative is
then the cross-flight medoid in normalized lift/drag response, so one outlying
flight cannot acquire the confidence supplied by many agreeing flights.

Independent-flight support controls trust. Raw packet frequency does not.

### 2. Frozen nominal model during entry

Current-flight force vectors no longer edit the operational direct-force table.
They are persisted for later use and compared against the current data book to
produce a model residual. The residual can warn the operator and widen robust
planning assumptions, but it does not move the nominal force model during the
same descent.

The former Mach-binned adaptive L/D/ballistic calibrator remains available as a
shadow estimator and review aid. Automatic in-flight application is limited to
operational speed margins; it cannot silently rewrite entry L/D or ballistic
coefficient. Explicitly applying a reviewed calibration invalidates the current
deorbit plan and requires replanning.

### 3. Scheduled fallback envelope plus local direct-force cells

Clean prior-flight cells derive a four-regime Mach-scheduled L/D and ballistic
fallback envelope. Local direct-force cells remain preferred when covered. This
is not intended to reproduce the Shuttle Data Book's exact independent variables;
it is the smallest feasible KSP representation that preserves regime scheduling
and configuration separation without inventing fixed vehicle coefficients.

Gear, wheel brakes and speedbrake state are separate direct-force configurations.
Unknown legacy speedbrake state is retained for forensics but is not an
operational prior.

### 4. Shadow flight-test reconstruction and deliberate excitation

Normal current-flight data is recorded passively. The explicit glide calibration
mode is treated as a flight-test maneuver. Its AoA sweep is bounded by the
existing safety gates, and one short speedbrake-on window is permitted only in a
high-margin part of the middle-AoA sample. This gathers the missing deployed-
speedbrake response for a later session without changing the current session's
nominal model.

Recovery/abort observations remain raw forensic data and are excluded from
nominal certification.

### 5. RCS/aerosurface authority blending

The bridge no longer commands full RCS simply because Mach is above one fixed
threshold. Measured pitch/roll/yaw aerodynamic ownership determines how much
backup RCS authority is required. As surfaces demonstrate authority, the RCS
share fades out axis-by-axis. A final transonic latch prevents weak/noisy
identification from rearming RCS late in TAEM or approach.

This intentionally copies the Shuttle's *architecture* rather than its numeric
q-bar thresholds: STS-N on Kerbin has different mass, atmosphere, control
surfaces, reaction-control geometry and KSP actuator semantics.

### 6. TAEM q-bar/energy speedbrake control

The previous simple hot/not-hot speedbrake trigger has been replaced in TAEM by
a normalized q-bar PI controller with a specific-energy term and binary
hysteresis. This is the feasible KSP analogue of the Shuttle TAEM speedbrake
law. KSP exposes the configured airbrake action group as a binary device, so a
Schmitt trigger replaces the Orbiter's continuous surface-position command.

Automatic airborne use is forbidden unless the data book has local
speedbrake-deployed coverage. This is important for STS-N: at the time of this
change the main structure/environment archive contained thousands of clean
observations across dozens of sessions but no known speedbrake-deployed rows.
Until a calibration pulse is flown and a later session reloads it, airborne
automatic speedbrake use stays inhibited. Rollout may still deploy it because
airborne trajectory prediction is no longer involved.

### 7. Explicit aerodynamic robustness cases

Deorbit robustness evaluation now includes drag-high/lift-low and
drag-low/lift-high cases in addition to timing, thrust, delta-v, mass, state,
pointing and atmosphere dispersions. Their aerodynamic magnitude is based on
prior-flight scatter and may be enlarged by a confident current-flight model
residual. Stress factors are scenario-local and never alter the nominal data
book.

### 8. Reduced-order prediction retained deliberately

NASA planning tools did not require a full onboard six-degree-of-freedom
parameter estimator for every guidance evaluation. The present predictor keeps
its reduced translational/attitude-response model, but its attitude response is
fed by measured authority and its device configuration is now propagated through
TAEM. This gives the landing program a tractable receding-horizon model without
pretending that every unmeasured aerodynamic moment can be identified online.

## What was deliberately not copied

The Shuttle's exact dynamic-pressure thresholds, speedbrake angle schedules,
air-data architecture, redundant flight computers, hydraulic actuator models,
body-flap/elevon trim schedules and Earth atmosphere tables are vehicle-specific
and are not appropriate KSP constants.

The real rudder/speedbrake had a continuously scheduled position; the KSP action
group is binary. The KSP controller therefore keeps the same q-bar/energy
feedback concept but uses hysteresis and requires configuration-specific data.

The current KSP predictor is still not a complete 6-DoF aeroelastic simulator.
Sideslip evolution, aerodynamic moment tables, inertia cross-products, actuator
delay and damage/topology changes remain outside its present reduced model.
